Is our Prometheus model built exactly like the research paper?

A jargon-free walkthrough of how our code compares to the paper it is based on, what we just changed to close the last gap, and the two honest caveats you should know about.

The short answer

Yes — the model's design is now an exact copy of the paper.

There was one remaining gap, and it was not in the design — it was in the training routine (how the model is taught). Until today, our code could not even be made to train exactly like the paper, because of one specific habit it always followed. We have now added a single switch, --paper-exact, that turns off all of our extra habits and reproduces the paper's routine step-for-step. So: the design already matched, and now the whole thing can match on demand.

🎂 A simple way to picture it. Think of the paper as a prize-winning cake recipe.

  • The "architecture" is the recipe itself — the ingredients and the method. We checked every ingredient and every step against the paper, and they now match exactly.
  • The "training" is the baking — the oven temperature, the timer, and whether you keep the cake straight out of the oven or fuss with it. The paper says: bake for a set time, no peeking, use the cake as-is at the end.
  • Our kitchen had picked up some helpful habits — an oven that auto-adjusts its own heat, taking the cake out early if a probe says it looks done, and keeping the best-looking cake out of several checks. Those habits usually give a nicer cake, but they are not what the recipe says.
  • The new --paper-exact switch is a "recipe-exact" button: it turns every habit off and bakes precisely as the paper instructs.

What the paper is

Our model is a re-build of a 2025 research paper by Auttapon Pomsathit (KMITL, Thailand), published in IEEE Access. The paper describes a system that spots cyber-attacks by looking at a network as a graph — a web of connected devices — and deciding, for each device, whether it is behaving maliciously. It also has a "memory" trick so it keeps recognising old attacks while learning new ones.

Our job has been to make sure our version is faithful to that paper, so that when we compare scores, we are comparing like with like.

Two honest asterisks on the word "exact"

These are not mistakes in our code. They are two places where the paper itself doesn't say, so "exact" can only mean "faithful to what the paper actually states":

  • How many attention "viewpoints" (heads). The paper says it uses attention, but never says how many parallel viewpoints. Our code uses four — a standard, sensible choice. You can't exactly copy a number the paper never prints.
  • Per-device vs whole-network verdict. The paper is internally inconsistent: its equations describe a per-device verdict (which we follow), but one of its data tables implies a single whole-network verdict. We follow the equations, and keep the other reading available as an option.

Everything the paper does pin down, we match exactly. These two items are unset or contradictory in the source paper, not choices we got wrong.

One distinction to keep straight

  • The model's design (everything in the checklist above) is exact out of the box — no flags.
  • The training routine matches the paper only when you add --paper-exact. By default we use an enhanced routine; the switch locks it to the paper. (Details in the next two sections.)

What we changed today

We added one switch to the training program: --paper-exact. When you use it, the program:

  • turns off the auto-adjusting learning speed;
  • turns off early stopping, so it always does the full 200 rounds;
  • keeps the final trained model (the paper's way) instead of a best-mid-practice one — this is the behaviour that simply did not exist before;
  • makes its yes/no decision the same plain way the paper does;
  • and refuses to run if you accidentally also ask for a non-paper upgrade, so you can never get a half-and-half result by mistake.

We verified all of this with an automated self-test that trains a small model end-to-end, saves it, reloads it, and confirms every setting is exactly the paper's. The self-test passed.

How to run the paper-exact version

python scripts/train_prometheus.py \
    --snapshots-root data/snapshots \
    --train-scenarios ctu13_c52,ctu13_c46,ctu13_c53,ctu13_c48,ctu13_c45,ctu13_c49,ctu13_c54 \
    --test-scenarios  ctu13_c47 \
    --paper-exact --seeds 42,43,44,45,46 \
    --out models/checkpoints/prometheus_paper_c47

Without --paper-exact, you get the same paper-faithful design plus our modern training habits. With it, you get the paper's method top to bottom.

Two honest caveats

Caveat 1 — Our old score looked better than it really was

An earlier result made the model look almost perfect (around 99%). That number was misleading. It was measured on a tiny, very lopsided sample where about 96 out of every 100 items were attacks — so even a broken model that just shouted "attack!" at everything would have scored about 96%. The "99%" was barely better than that.

The fix is the same one that made the design match the paper: judging each device individually gives us hundreds of fair test cases instead of a handful of lopsided ones, so future scores will be honest. The takeaway: our real performance is more modest than that old number suggested — and now we will measure it fairly.

Caveat 2 — The current data can't teach the model anything yet

Right now, the data we feed the model gives every device the same blank name-tag. Asking the model to tell attackers apart from this is like asking someone to pick the culprit out of a line-up where everyone is wearing an identical mask — it's impossible, no matter how clever they are.

This is a data preparation issue, not a flaw in the model's design. Before any serious training, we need to rebuild the data so each device carries real, descriptive information. That rebuild is a cheap, standard step — it just hasn't been run yet.

What happens next

Three steps remain. All of them are on hold until you give the go-ahead, because the last one costs money to run on rented GPUs:

  1. Rebuild the data with real device features (cheap, no GPU). Fixes caveat 2.
  2. Check how densely connected the real network is (cheap, no GPU). Tells us whether the optional "shortcut wire" upgrade is worth switching on.
  3. Run the full paper-exact training on a GPU, five times, and report the honest average. This is the one that costs money.

Nothing has been run on paid hardware for this report. Everything above was checked on this machine at no cost. The paid GPU run waits for your explicit "go".
